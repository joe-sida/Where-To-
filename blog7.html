Exploring East Hararghe: A Hidden Gem in Ethiopia

Perched on a highland plateau where the Ethiopian highlands tumble into the arid lowlands of the Ogaden, East Hararghe is a region of dizzying contrasts. Misty green slopes are terraced with chat trees as far as the eye can see, their tender leaves shimmering silver in the morning sun. Here, the air smells of wild mint, roasting coffee, and the dry breath of the desert beyond. This is a land of ancient trade routes, where Somali camel caravans once met Oromo farmers, where Islam has flourished for centuries, and where every visit feels like stepping into a living manuscript. Welcome to one of Ethiopia's most underrated corners — raw, ritualistic, and utterly unforgettable.

The Walled City of a Thousand Alleyways

At the heart of East Hararghe lies Harar Jugol, the fabled walled city that UNESCO calls the fourth-holiest city of Islam. Step through one of its five ancient gates — Duke's Gate is the usual entry — and you're swallowed by a labyrinth of twisting alleyways no wider than a man's outstretched arms. Over 368 distinct alleys snake between whitewashed houses with brightly painted doors, each neighborhood a mosaic of Somali, Oromo, Harari, and Arabic influence. Women in vibrant dirac and shash glide past men in kufi caps carrying bundles of chat. Minarets pierce the skyline alongside the domes of 82 mosques and dozens of shrines. The city is a sensory overload: the scent of frankincense wafts from doorways, the crash of metal from coppersmith quarters, the singsong call to prayer echoing off mud-plastered walls. Getting lost here is not a mistake — it's the whole point.

The Hyena Man: Feeding the Spirits of the Night

Just outside Duke's Gate, as dusk paints the sky deep indigo, a tradition that predates Islam and Christianity unfolds. The hyena men of Harar — a hereditary role passed down through generations — emerge with a basket of raw meat on their backs and a stick between their teeth. They call into the darkness with a strange, guttural chant, and soon the hyenas slink out of the wadis, eyes glowing like amber coals. These are not caged animals or circus tricks; they are wild spotted hyenas, some weighing over 60 kilograms, that come within arm's reach to take meat from the man's mouth or hand. The most famous hyena man, Abbas Yusuf — now in his seventies — has been feeding them since 1964, a living link to a practice believed to appease the spirits of the dead and protect the city from harm. For visitors, it's a spine-tingling encounter that feels less like a show and more like bearing witness to an ancient, unbroken covenant between man and beast.

Where Coffee Was Born and Khat Reigns Supreme

East Hararghe lives and breathes two leaf-based economies: coffee and khat. Drive through the highlands around Dire Dawa or Harar itself and you'll see terraced hillsides carpeted in qat (or chat, as it's locally known), the evergreen shrub whose tender tips are chewed daily by millions across the Horn of Africa. But to dismiss the region as merely a khat hub would be a traveler's mistake. East Hararghe claims a role in coffee's origin story — the Sufi saint Ali ibn Omar al-Shadhili is said to have discovered coffee in the highlands here, and the tradition lives on in every home. No visit to Harar is complete without attending a coffee ceremony, where beans are roasted over charcoal in a pan, ground with a pestle, and brewed in a clay jebena. The ritual unfolds over an hour, three rounds of cups, and endless conversation. Try the coffee with a pinch of salt or a spoonful of ghee — it's how the elders prefer it.

How to Visit East Hararghe

Getting to East Hararghe is part of the adventure. Fly from Addis Ababa to Dire Dawa (an hour on Ethiopian Airlines), then take a taxi or local minibus the 55 kilometers to Harar through winding escarpment roads. Budget a minimum of three days: one to lose yourself in the old city, one for the hyena feeding at sunset and a coffee ceremony at a traditional Harari home, and one to explore the surrounding highland villages of Jarar and Kombolcha for the best views of the rift valley escarpment. Dry season (October through March) offers the clearest skies and the most comfortable trekking. The local currency is birr — bring enough cash, as ATMs are scarce. Accommodation ranges from the heritage-heavy Heritage Plaza Hotel inside the walls to family-run guesthouses in the old town. And yes, a guide helps, but wandering without one is where the real magic hides.

East Hararghe doesn't flatter the casual tourist. It doesn't have the polish of Lalibela's rock churches or the wildlife fame of the Omo Valley. What it offers is rarer: a place where you can stand inside a living, breathing medieval city, feed a hyena in the dark, watch chat farmers descend misty terraces at dawn, and sip the same coffee that made a Sufi mystic dance — all in the same day. The Hyena Man will eventually pass his meat basket to a successor. The ancient walls will keep standing. But you? You'll never be the same traveler who walked through those gates.
